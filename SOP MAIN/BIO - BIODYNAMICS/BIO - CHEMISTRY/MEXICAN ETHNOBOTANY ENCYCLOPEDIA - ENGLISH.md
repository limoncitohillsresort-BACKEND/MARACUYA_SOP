### **`Guazuma ulmifolia` 
Lam.** — *Malvaceae (formerly Sterculiaceae)* — **Guácima, Pixoy (Maya), Cuaulote**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native from Mexico through tropical South America; abundant in Pacific and Gulf tropical dry forest, including Nayarit.
- **Part used:** Bark, leaves, fruit.
- **Identification:** Small-to-medium deciduous tree (to ~15 m); alternate, serrate, ovate-oblong leaves with **stellate pubescence** and oblique leaf base; small yellowish cymose flowers; distinctive **warty, hard, globose capsule** (~2–3 cm) that persists on the tree. Bark grey, fissured.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Bark/fruit decoction taken for acute diarrhea and dysentery — a use consistent with its high **tannin / proanthocyanidin (procyanidin)** content, which precipitates proteins (astringent, antimicrobial adhesion) and reduces gut motility.
- **Active compounds:** Proanthocyanidins, catechin/epicatechin, tannins, mucilage, kaurane diterpenes, caffeine-related bases (trace).
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical* for antidiarrheal, hypoglycemic, and antimicrobial activity; hypotensive/vasorelaxant effects reported in animal models.
- ***Note (misconception):*** The source's claim that it is a "hair-loss remedy" is **folkloric, not supported** — mucilage conditions hair cosmetically but does not treat alopecia.

**USAGE**
- **Targets (documented):** Acute diarrhea, dysentery, dermatologic astringent use.
- **Route:** Oral decoction; topical wash.
- **Dosage/processing:** *General reference only* — traditionally a bark decoction; precise self-treatment dosing not provided.
- **Overdose:** High-tannin intake → nausea, constipation, impaired mineral/iron absorption on chronic use.
- **Interactions:** Tannins impair absorption of iron and alkaloid/aminoglycoside drugs; separate dosing.
- **Caution:** Generally low acute toxicity. **Tier B.**

### **`Amphipterygium adstringens` 
(Schltdl.) Standl.** — *Anacardiaceae* — **Cuachalalate, Cuachalalatl (Nahuatl)**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Endemic to Mexican tropical dry forest (Balsas basin, Pacific slope, central Mexico).
- **Part used:** Stem bark.
- **Identification:** Dioecious tree to ~10 m; thick, deeply fissured greyish bark; imparipinnate leaves clustered at branch tips; small flowers; **winged samara-like fruit**. Bark intensely astringent.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Bark decoction for gastric ulcers, gastritis, oral inflammation — supported by **anacardic acids, masticadienonic and 3α-hydroxymasticadienonic acids, triterpenes**, which show gastroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and anti-*Helicobacter pylori* activity.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical* (gastroprotective, antihyperlipidemic, anti-*H. pylori*); some human traditional-clinical observational use.
- **Active compounds:** Anacardic acids, masticadienonic acid, triterpenes, tannins.
- ***Note:*** One of the better-substantiated Mexican gastroprotectants; overharvest of wild trees is a conservation concern.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Gastritis, peptic ulcer, mouth/gum inflammation, hypercholesterolemia.
- **Route:** Oral decoction; mouth rinse.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only — bark decoction traditionally.
- **Overdose:** GI upset; anacardic acids are related to urushiol — **contact dermatitis** possible in sensitized individuals.
- **Interactions:** Possible additive with lipid-lowering/antiplatelet agents.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.** Conservation-sensitive.

### **`Heliopsis longipes` 
(A.Gray) S.F.Blake** — *Asteraceae* — **Chilcuague, Raíz de Oro / Aztec Gold Root, Pelitre**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Endemic to the Sierra Gorda convergence (Guanajuato, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí) — **not coastal Nayarit** (a correction to the source).
- **Part used:** Root.
- **Identification:** Perennial herb; opposite lanceolate-ovate leaves; solitary yellow ray-and-disc capitula on long peduncles ("longipes" = long-footed); fibrous aromatic root that produces intense oral tingling/numbing and salivation on chewing.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Chewed for toothache and oral infection — **affinin (spilanthol)**, an N-isobutylamide alkylamide, produces local paresthesia/analgesia and pungency, with documented antimicrobial/antifungal and insecticidal activity.
- **Active compounds:** Affinin (spilanthol) and related N-alkylamides.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical* strong for antifungal, antimicrobial, insecticidal, local-anesthetic-like, and vasorelaxant activity.
- ***Note:*** The source's chemistry here is **largely accurate** — a rare bright spot. Sodium-channel modulation is plausible but not fully established mechanistically.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Toothache, oral/throat infection, topical antifungal.
- **Route:** Masticatory (chewing root fragment), topical.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** GI irritation, excessive salivation, nausea in quantity.
- **Interactions:** Limited data.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.** Endemic/overharvested — **conservation-sensitive (borderline E)**.

### **`Justicia spicigera` 
Schltdl.** — *Acanthaceae* — **Muicle, Mohuitli (Nahuatl), Muitle**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native Mexico–Central America.
- **Part used:** Leaves, aerial parts.
- **Identification:** Shrub to ~1.5 m; opposite entire lanceolate leaves; tubular **orange-red** flowers in spikes; aqueous extract fluoresces **blue-green**.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** "Blood tonic" for anemia and post-illness recovery — contains **kaempferitrin, flavonoids, phenols, and alkaloids**; shown to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and (preclinically) hypoglycemic and cytotoxic activity.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical.*
- ***Note (misconception):*** No good evidence it "stimulates erythrocyte synthesis." The "builds blood" reputation is **traditional, not hematologically demonstrated**.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Fatigue/"weak blood," skin conditions, menstrual complaints (traditional).
- **Route:** Oral infusion; topical.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** Low reported toxicity.
- **Interactions:** Possible additive hypoglycemic effect.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.**

### **`Hintonia latiflora` 
(Sessé & Moc. ex DC.) Bullock** — *Rubiaceae* — **Copalquín, Quina del País**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Mexican tropical dry forest (Pacific slope, Sonora to Oaxaca).
- **Part used:** Inner bark.
- **Identification:** Tree/shrub with pale bark; opposite leaves; large white showy corolla ("latiflora"); intensely bitter bark.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Antipyretic and for type-2 diabetes — contains **coutareagenin and other 4-phenylcoumarins/neoflavonoids**; preclinical and some small clinical studies support modest glucose-lowering / α-glucosidase inhibition.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical* + limited *clinical* (small trials of standardized bark extract, mostly European) for adjunct glycemic support.
- ***Note (misconception):*** "Antimalarial like quinine" is **name-based folklore** — it is not *Cinchona* and has no quinine; the "quina" name reflects bitterness, not shared chemistry.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Adjunct glycemic support, fever (traditional).
- **Route:** Oral bark decoction/standardized extract.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** GI upset; **hypoglycemia risk if combined with antidiabetic drugs**.
- **Interactions:** Additive with insulin/sulfonylureas/metformin — monitor.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.**

### **`Bursera simaruba` 
(L.) Sarg.** — *Burseraceae* — **Palo Mulato, Chacah (Maya), Cuajiote**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native Mexico–Caribbean–Central America.
- **Part used:** Bark, resin, leaves.
- **Identification:** The **"gumbo-limbo / tourist tree"** — smooth, coppery-red, **peeling papery bark**; aromatic resin; pinnate leaves.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Anti-inflammatory, and famously the traditional antidote-poultice for **`Metopium brownei` (chechén)** contact dermatitis (the two trees often grow together). Contains **α-pinene, limonene, lupeol, β-sitosterol, lignans**; anti-inflammatory/antihistaminic activity is plausible and partly supported.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical*; chechén-antidote use is well-documented traditionally.
- ***Note (misconception):*** As a **snakebite cure it is ineffective** — envenomation is a medical emergency requiring antivenom; do not rely on any plant.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Contact dermatitis (esp. chechén), inflammation, fever, wounds.
- **Route:** Topical bark/leaf; oral decoction (traditional).
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** Low toxicity.
- **Interactions:** Minimal known.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.** Do not substitute for snakebite antivenom.

### **`Psidium guajava` 
L.** — *Myrtaceae* — **Guayaba, Guayabo, Pichi'/Enандir**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native tropical America (incl. Mexico); pantropical now.
- **Part used:** Leaves (primary medicinal), fruit.
- **Identification:** Shrub/small tree; smooth mottled bark; opposite elliptic leaves with prominent pinnate venation; white 5-petaled flowers; aromatic berry.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Leaf decoction for acute diarrhea — **quercetin, avicularin, guaijaverin, tannins** produce antispasmodic (Ca²⁺-channel-related) and antimicrobial effects. This is one of the **best clinically-supported** traditional antidiarrheals.
- **Evidence:** *Clinical* (leaf-extract trials for acute diarrhea) + preclinical.
- **Active compounds:** Quercetin glycosides, tannins, essential oils, triterpenes.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Acute diarrhea, dysentery, oral inflammation.
- **Route:** Oral leaf infusion/decoction.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** Constipation, tannin effects.
- **Interactions:** May mildly affect glucose; tannin–drug absorption.
- **Caution:** **Tier A–B.**

### **`Casimiroa edulis` 
La Llave & Lex.** — *Rutaceae* — **Zapote Blanco, Iztactzapotl (Nahuatl)**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native central/southern Mexico highlands.
- **Part used:** Leaves, seeds (seeds toxic — see note).
- **Identification:** Evergreen tree; palmately compound leaves (usually 5 leaflets); greenish-yellow edible fruit with sweet custardy pulp; **seeds bitter and toxic**.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Leaf infusion for insomnia and hypertension — leaves/seeds contain **N-benzoyltyramine derivatives, casimiroin, coumarins, histamine-related amides, imidazole alkaloids**; hypotensive and sedative effects reported preclinically.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical.*
- ***Note (correction):*** The source's "dimethylhistamine → vasodilation" gloss is oversimplified/unreliable. **Seeds are toxic** and should not be eaten; the fruit *pulp* is safe food.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Insomnia, mild hypertension, anxiety (traditional — leaf).
- **Route:** Oral leaf infusion.
- **Dosage/processing:** Seeds `[—REDACTED—]` (toxic; no home preparation).
- **Overdose:** Seed ingestion → toxicity; excessive leaf → hypotension, sedation.
- **Interactions:** Additive with antihypertensives/sedatives.
- **Caution:** Leaf **Tier B**; seed **Tier C**.

### **`Agave` spp. (incl. `
A. tequilana` F.A.C.Weber)** — *Asparagaceae* — **Maguey, Metl (Nahuatl)**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Mexican endemic centers of diversity.
- **Part used:** Sap (aguamiel), leaf, roasted heart (piña).
- **Identification:** Rosette succulent; thick fibrous leaves with terminal spine and marginal teeth; monocarpic tall inflorescence.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Aguamiel/roasted sap for wounds and digestion — **agavins (fructans/prebiotics)**, saponins, and fructooligosaccharides support gut-flora and mild antimicrobial effects.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical* (prebiotic, wound-supportive).
- ***Note:*** Raw sap **saponins are irritant**; "mezcal-filled gourd cough syrups" are cultural, not clinically validated.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Wounds, digestive/prebiotic support.
- **Route:** Topical; oral (fermented/cooked).
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** Raw sap → mucosal/skin irritation (calcium oxalate + saponins).
- **Interactions:** Minimal.
- **Caution:** **Tier A–B** (fermented alcoholic products carry their own risks).

### **`Tagetes lucida` 
Cav.** — *Asteraceae* — **Pericón, Yauhtli (Nahuatl), Hierba de San Juan**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native Mexico/Central America.
- **Part used:** Aerial parts, flowers.
- **Identification:** Aromatic (anise/tarragon scent) perennial; linear-lanceolate serrate leaves with translucent oil glands; small golden-yellow capitula.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Carminative/antispasmodic tea, ritual protective herb — **coumarins, methyleugenol, estragole, flavonoids**; antispasmodic and sedative activity preclinically.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical.*
- ***Note (caution):*** Contains **estragole/methyleugenol**, which are hepatocarcinogenic in rodents at high chronic doses — occasional culinary/tea use is traditional; chronic high intake is discouraged.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Colic, "susto," GI spasm (traditional).
- **Route:** Oral infusion; ritual smoke.
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference only.
- **Overdose:** GI upset; sedation.
- **Interactions:** Additive sedative; theoretical hepatotoxic potentiation.
- **Caution:** **Tier B.**

### **`Moringa oleifera` 
Lam.** — *Moringaceae* — **Moringa, Árbol de las baquetas**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Native to the Indian subcontinent; introduced to Pacific Mexico (Manila Galleon era), naturalized.
- **Part used:** Leaves, seeds, pods.
- **Identification:** Fast-growing tree; tripinnate leaves with small obovate leaflets; fragrant white flowers; long pendulous three-angled pods.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Nutritional leaf; seeds as **coagulant water clarifier** (a genuine, documented use — cationic proteins flocculate turbidity, though they do **not** disinfect microbiologically). Leaves rich in vitamins, minerals, **glucosinolate-derived isothiocyanates** with anti-inflammatory/antioxidant activity.
- **Evidence:** *Clinical/nutritional* (leaf); *documented* (seed water treatment); *preclinical* (metabolic).
- ***Note (correction):*** Seeds clarify but do **not** "purify" water of pathogens.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Malnutrition, dietary supplementation.
- **Route:** Oral (leaf); mechanical (seed).
- **Dosage/processing:** General reference; **root/root-bark not for consumption** (contains spirochin — potentially toxic).
- **Overdose:** Leaf: laxative in excess. Root: `[—REDACTED—]` (avoid).
- **Interactions:** Possible additive hypoglycemic/antihypertensive; theoretical thyroid effects with excess.
- **Caution:** Leaf **Tier A–B**; root **Tier C**.

### **`Ruta graveolens` 
L.** — *Rutaceae* — **Ruda, Rue**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Mediterranean; introduced, naturalized in Mexican gardens.
- **Part used:** Aerial parts (with caution).
- **Identification:** Strongly scented subshrub; bluish-green bipinnate leaves with glandular dots; yellow 4–5-petaled flowers.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** *limpias*, and — dangerously — as an emmenagogue/abortifacient. Contains **rutin, furanocoumarins (bergapten, psoralen), quinoline alkaloids, essential oils**.
- **Evidence:** *Preclinical*; toxicology *well documented*.
- ***Note (serious warning):*** Its use to "bring on menses"/induce abortion is **a leading cause of Rue poisoning** — hepatotoxicity, GI hemorrhage, and death have occurred. Furanocoumarins cause **severe phototoxic burns** on skin contact + sun.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** (Traditional) menstrual, earache, ritual — **not recommended internally**.
- **Route:** Historically oral/topical.
- **Dosage/processing:** `[—REDACTED—]` (abortifacient/toxic dosing withheld).
- **Overdose:** Vomiting, cramping, hepatotoxicity, seizures, hemorrhage.
- **Interactions:** Phototoxic; potentiates photosensitizing drugs; CYP interactions.
- **Caution:** **Tier C–D.** Contraindicated in pregnancy.

### **`Azadirachta indica` 
A.Juss.** — *Meliaceae* — **Neem, Nim**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Indian subcontinent; naturalized around San Blas and tropical Mexico.

### **`Ricinus communis` 
L.** — *Euphorbiaceae* — **Higuerilla, Castor / Ricino**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Africa/Asia origin; naturalized weed throughout Mexico.
- **Part used:** Cold-pressed seed oil (medicinal); leaves (topical).
- **Identification:** Robust herb/shrub; large palmate 7–11-lobed leaves (often reddish); spiny capsules; mottled beanlike seeds.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Castor **oil** as stimulant laxative — **ricinoleic acid** activates intestinal EP₃ prostaglandin receptors.
- ***Note (critical):*** The **seeds contain `ricin`**, one of the most lethal plant toxins known. Properly processed oil is ricin-free; **raw seeds are deadly.**
- **Evidence:** *Clinical* (oil, laxative); ricin toxicology *well established*.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Constipation (oil), topical inflammation (leaf).
- **Route:** Oral (oil only), topical (leaf).
- **Dosage/processing:** Seed processing `[—REDACTED—]`. Do not ingest seeds.
- **Overdose:** Oil: cramping, dehydration, electrolyte loss. **Seed: ricin poisoning — multi-organ failure, often fatal.**
- **Interactions:** Electrolyte/diuretic; drug malabsorption.
- **Caution:** Oil **Tier B**; **seed Tier D.**

### **`Aloe vera` 
(L.) Burm.f.** — *Asphodelaceae* — **Sábila**

**PLANT DATA**
- **Native range:** Arabian Peninsula origin; ubiquitously cultivated in Mexico.
- **Part used:** Inner leaf gel (topical), latex (caution).
- **Identification:** Stemless succulent rosette; thick lanceolate leaves with marginal teeth; clear mucilaginous inner gel + yellow bitter latex layer.

**BODY DATA**
- **Traditional use vs. mechanism:** Gel for burns/wounds — **acemannan (polysaccharide)** supports wound healing; **anthraquinones (aloin, barbaloin)** in the latex are stimulant laxatives.
- **Evidence:** *Clinical* (topical burn/wound), *clinical* (aloin laxative — but discouraged).
- ***Note:*** Oral latex (aloin) is a harsh laxative and possible carcinogen concern with chronic use; use decolorized/gel products only.

**USAGE**
- **Targets:** Minor burns, wounds, skin.
- **Route:** Topical (gel).
- **Dosage/processing:** Latex/oral `[—REDACTED—]`.
- **Overdose:** Latex → cramping, electrolyte loss, dependency.
- **Interactions:** Latex + diuretics/digoxin (hypokalemia risk).
- **Caution:** Gel **Tier A**; latex **Tier C.**

### **`Lophophora williamsii` 
(Lem. ex Salm-Dyck) J.M.Coult.** — *Cactaceae* — **Peyote, Híkuri (Wixárika)**

### **`Turbina corymbosa`
